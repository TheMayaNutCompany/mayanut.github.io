About The MayaNut Company

Below is the original English story of MayaNut and Brosimum alicastrum, kept intact so global collaborators and researchers can read the base version even from the Japanese page.

About The MayaNut Company

Welcome to The MayaNut Company at mayanut.com, created to bring a single underused tree – Brosimum alicastrum – into the spotlight as a true tropical superfood. In Spanish it is often called nuez de ramón or ojoche. In other regions it goes by breadnut, guayumaro, guaimaro, and more. On this site we unify those traditions under one simple name: MayaNut.

People have eaten the fruits and seeds of this tree for centuries, roasting the “nuts” into a coffee-like drink, grinding them into flour, or storing them as emergency food. Our goal is to translate that history into a modern industry built around respectful agriculture, genetics, and world-class flavor.

Founder – August Kokus

The MayaNut Company was founded in 2025 by August Kokus, a South Floridian botanist fascinated by banyan trees, figs, and the entire Moraceae family. While exploring this family, he found Brosimum alicastrum – a tree whose fruits resemble a small fig, but whose seed has an entirely different potential.

August wanted to build something that could live on after him: a planted, living world of trees that people actually use. MayaNut offered exactly that – a species with genuine food value, deep cultural history, and enough mystery that almost no one in the United States had explored it seriously.

By around 2030, he aims to have a functional plantation of MayaNut trees in South Florida, at least an acre in size, bearing the first real harvests that can be turned into beverages, powders, flours, and more.

Where We’re Growing

South Florida’s warm, frost-free climate makes it a natural testing ground for Brosimum alicastrum. The MayaNut Company focuses first on locations at or below roughly 27°N in Florida, where winter cold is rare and evergreen trees can maintain year-round growth.

Further north – up toward 28°N – the species may survive but still needs long-term trials. Our early plantings explore which microclimates, soils, and layouts give the tree the best chance to become a stable, hurricane-resilient part of the landscape.

Mission & Long View

Our mission is simple and ambitious at the same time: make Brosimum alicastrum impossible to ignore. That means developing an entire chain – from genetics and seedlings, to plantations and agroforestry, to MayaNut superfood powders and beverages on store shelves.

We want future generations to recognize MayaNut the way people recognize coffee or cacao today: as a flavor, a story, and a living tree that shaped landscapes and communities. This site is the first chapter in that story.

MAYANUT 研究セクション – BROSIMUM ALICASTRUM

MayaNut Research & Tree Background

This section keeps an English snapshot of what is known – and still being learned – about Brosimum alicastrum and the MayaNut seed, so that researchers can reference it even from the Japanese page.

The leaves of Brosimum alicastrum tell part of the story: a dense, evergreen canopy built for shade, rain, and long-term resilience in tropical and subtropical landscapes. All of the sections below grow out of that fact – a robust tree carrying a powerful food seed.

Across its native range, people know this species under many names: MayaNut, nuez de ramón, ojoche, breadnut, guayumaro, guaimaro, and more. On this site we use the unified term MayaNut for the seed while still honoring those local names.

mayanut superfood nuez de ramón ojoche breadnut tropical canopy tree

Common Names & Indigenous Use

Brosimum alicastrum has many names. In English and Spanish it is often called MayaNut, nuez de ramón, or ojoche. Elsewhere it may be known as breadnut, guayumaro, or guaimaro. All point to one idea: a canopy tree whose fruits and seeds can feed people and animals.

Roasted seeds can make a warm drink similar to a caffeine-free coffee or cocoa, or be ground into flour for tortillas, breads, and porridges. In some communities, ramón / ojoche served as a famine-resilient food when other crops failed.

Range, Climate & Florida Trials

In the wild, Brosimum alicastrum grows from southern Mexico through Central America into northern South America. It tolerates both humid rainforest and seasonal dry forests.

Once established, deep roots allow the tree to withstand dry spells and storms. This resilience is why MayaNut is being trialed in South Florida as a long-lived, hurricane-aware superfood canopy.

Growth, Roots & Structure

MayaNut trees can reach 20–40 m with a broad crown and dense evergreen foliage. Young trees invest heavily in roots and trunk before dramatic canopy expansion.

Root systems often lock into rocky or limestone-rich soils, acting as living anchors that stabilize slopes and help the tree ride out high winds.

Agroforestry & Superfood Systems

Traditionally, Brosimum alicastrum appears as scattered shade over crops or grazing. It can share space with cacao, coffee, fruit trees, or pasture, providing leaf litter, shade, and structure.

For modern agroforestry, it can serve as a superfood anchor tree – a permanent upper layer above crops that may change over time.

Genetics & Domestication

The species remains early in domestication compared with mango or citrus. There are at least two recognized subspecies – subsp. alicastrum and subsp. bolivarense.

The MayaNut Company aims to keep a broad genetic base: multiple seedlines from different regions, tracked and documented for future growers and researchers.

Directive: Survival & Visibility

Every MayaNut tree planted is part of a larger directive: make sure Brosimum alicastrum survives and thrives at both the genetic and academic level.

This website acts as a living notebook where observations, trials, and real-world data can be recorded and built upon for decades.

MAYANUT RECIPES – 飲料・ポリッジ・フラットブレッド

MayaNut Recipes

A few simple starting ideas for using MayaNut powder and fruit: drinks, porridge, flatbreads, and a fruit jelly based on the sweet pulp.

Drink · Mocha

Hot / Iced MayaNut Mocha

Combine 1–2 tsp roasted MayaNut powder with cocoa, a pinch of sugar, and hot water or milk. Froth lightly. For iced, pour over ice with plant milk.

  • Evening-friendly, naturally caffeine-free.
  • Add coffee if you want a true hybrid latte.
Porridge

MayaNut Breakfast Porridge

Stir MayaNut powder into oatmeal or rice porridge at the end of cooking, with a little salt and fruit on top.

  • Works with water or milk-based porridges.
  • Use lightly roasted powder for a softer flavor.
Flatbread / Tortilla

MayaNut Flatbreads

Replace 5–20% of wheat flour or masa with MayaNut flour in simple flatbread or tortilla doughs.

  • Test small batches to find your preferred ratio.
  • Pairs well with chili, beans, and grilled vegetables.
Fruit · Jelly

MayaNut Fruit Jelly Concept

The ripe fruit pulp of Brosimum alicastrum can be simmered with sugar and citrus into a soft jelly, then served with toast or spooned into MayaNut porridges.

  • Use only fully ripe, aromatic fruits.
  • Strain if you prefer a smooth texture.
Cold · Latte

Cold MayaNut Latte

Shake MayaNut powder with cold oat or soy milk, a touch of vanilla, and ice. Optionally add a shot of espresso for a hybrid drink.

  • Serve in a clear glass to show color.
  • Adjust sweetness to local preference.
Future R&D

Future Recipe Space

As plantations mature, recipes will expand to include cookies, steamed breads, blended drinks with tropical fruit, and more region-specific dishes.

  • Goal: keep recipes simple, practical, and repeatable.
