About The MayaNut Company, Inc.

This project is not just a product idea. It is a living, multi-decade experiment in taking one underused tree – Brosimum alicastrum – and making it impossible to ignore in food, ecology, and culture.

What The MayaNut Company Is Doing

Welcome to The MayaNut Company, Inc. at mayanut.com, created to bring a single underused tree – Brosimum alicastrum – into the spotlight as a true tropical superfood. In Spanish it is often called nuez de ramón or ojoche. In other regions it goes by breadnut, guayumaro, guaimaro, and more. On this site we unify those traditions under one simple name: MayaNut.

People have eaten the fruits and seeds of this tree for centuries, roasting the “nuts” into a coffee-like drink, grinding them into flour, or storing them as emergency food. Our goal is to translate that history into a modern industry built around respectful agriculture, genetics, and world-class flavor.

One tree, many names – but the same core idea: a stable, canopy-level food source for a hotter, more unpredictable world.

Founder – August Kokus

The MayaNut Company was founded in 2025 by August Kokus, a South Floridian botanist fascinated by banyan trees, figs, and the entire Moraceae family. While exploring this family, he found Brosimum alicastrum – a tree whose fruits resemble a small fig, but whose seed has an entirely different potential.

August wanted to build something that could live on after him: a planted, living world of trees that people actually use. MayaNut offered exactly that – a species with genuine food value, deep cultural history, and enough mystery that almost no one in the United States had explored it seriously.

By around 2030, he aims to have a functional plantation of MayaNut trees in South Florida, at least an acre in size, bearing the first real harvests that can be turned into beverages, powders, flours, and more.

Where We’re Growing First

South Florida’s warm, frost-free climate makes it a natural testing ground for Brosimum alicastrum. The MayaNut Company focuses first on locations at or below roughly 27°N in Florida, where winter cold is rare and evergreen trees can maintain year-round growth.

Further north – up toward 28°N – the species may survive but still needs long-term trials. Our early plantings explore which microclimates, soils, and layouts give the tree the best chance to become a stable, hurricane-resilient part of the landscape.

Mission & Long View

Our mission is simple and ambitious at the same time: make Brosimum alicastrum impossible to ignore. That means developing an entire chain – from genetics and seedlings, to plantations and agroforestry, to MayaNut superfood powders and beverages on store shelves.

We want future generations to recognize MayaNut the way people recognize coffee or cacao today: as a flavor, a story, and a living tree that shaped landscapes and communities. This site is the first chapter in that story.

Nothing here is medical or nutritional advice – it is about a tree, its seed, and the possibility that one species can help reshape how we grow food in the tropics.

The MayaNut tree – Brosimum alicastrum

A canopy tree of the Moraceae family with fruits that look a bit like figs – but whose true value sits inside, in the starchy seed we call MayaNut. This section tracks names, range, ecology, and why the species matters for a hotter, less predictable century.

Names & family

Scientific name: Brosimum alicastrum, family Moraceae. Common names include MayaNut, nuez de ramón, ojoche, breadnut, guayumaro, and others across Mesoamerica and the Caribbean.

Moraceae · fig family Canopy tree MayaNut · seed

Distribution & climate

Native to tropical Mexico, Central America, and parts of northern South America. It prefers warm, frost-free climates and deep soils, but shows surprising drought and storm tolerance once established.

Tropical lowlands Hurricane-tested Evergreen canopy

Food & nutrition

The seed inside the fruit can be dried, roasted, and ground. Historically it has been used for drinks, porridges, flours, and emergency food during crop failures. Modern work is exploring its protein, fiber, and micronutrient profile.

Seed-based food Coffee-adjacent drink Flour & porridge

Ecology & agroforestry

As a tall canopy species with deep roots, Brosimum alicastrum can help stabilize slopes, shade understory crops, and store carbon. Its leaves and fruit also have roles in local wildlife diets and potential fodder systems.

Carbon storage Soil protection Agroforestry layers

Florida as a living lab

The MayaNut Company uses South Florida as a testbed: planting multiple seedlines, observing cold tolerance, storm survival, and growth speed, and comparing nursery and in-ground performance over many years.

We document which microclimates, rootstocks, and planting layouts show the most promise – data that will eventually flow into open notes and grower guides.

MayaNut recipe ideas

These are early concepts showing how roasted MayaNut seed might fit into everyday food and drink. Nothing here is medical or nutritional advice – it is about flavor, texture, and possibility.

Drink

Hot MayaNut mocha

Whisk roasted MayaNut powder with cocoa, a touch of sugar, and hot water or milk. Think “gentle coffee” – but with grainy depth and less edge.

Roasted seed Cocoa blend Comfort drink
Breakfast

MayaNut porridge

Stir MayaNut powder into oats or other grains while cooking. Finish with fruit, honey, or jam for a dense, warming breakfast bowl.

Porridge base Fruit topping Slow energy
Baking

Flatbreads & tortillas

Blend a portion of MayaNut flour into corn or wheat doughs. Test small batches for texture and flavor, focusing on mild, toasty notes.

Partial substitution Toasty flavor
Fruit & pulp

MayaNut fruit jam (concept)

Fruit pulp use Jam or jelly
