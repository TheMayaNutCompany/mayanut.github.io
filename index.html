ABOUT THE TREE · THE MAYANUT COMPANY STORY

About The MayaNut Company

The MayaNut Company is a long-term project built around a single underused tree: Brosimum alicastrum. The goal is to move this species from “forgotten” to “recognized” – as a real food crop, a genetic resource, and a future part of climate-resilient agriculture.

What is The MayaNut Company?

Welcome to The MayaNut Company at mayanut.com, created to bring a single underused tree – Brosimum alicastrum – into the spotlight as a true tropical superfood. In Spanish it is often called nuez de ramón or ojoche. In other regions it goes by breadnut, guayumaro, guaimaro, and more. On this site we unify those traditions under one simple name: MayaNut.

People have eaten the fruits and seeds of this tree for centuries, roasting the “nuts” into a coffee-like drink, grinding them into flour, or storing them as emergency food. Our goal is to translate that history into a modern industry built around respectful agriculture, genetics, and world-class flavor.

Founder – August Kokus

The MayaNut Company was founded in 2025 by August Kokus, a South Floridian botanist fascinated by banyan trees, figs, and the entire Moraceae family. While exploring this family, he found Brosimum alicastrum – a tree whose fruits resemble a small fig, but whose seed has an entirely different potential.

August wanted to build something that could live on after him: a planted, living world of trees that people actually use. MayaNut offered exactly that – a species with genuine food value, deep cultural history, and enough mystery that almost no one in the United States had explored it seriously.

By around 2030, he aims to have a functional plantation of MayaNut trees in South Florida, at least an acre in size, bearing the first real harvests that can be turned into beverages, powders, flours, and more.

Where We’re Growing

South Florida’s warm, frost-free climate makes it a natural testing ground for Brosimum alicastrum. The MayaNut Company focuses first on locations at or below roughly 27°N in Florida, where winter cold is rare and evergreen trees can maintain year-round growth.

Further north – up toward 28°N – the species may survive but still needs long-term trials. Our early plantings explore which microclimates, soils, and layouts give the tree the best chance to become a stable, hurricane-resilient part of the landscape.

Mission & Long View

Our mission is simple and ambitious at the same time: make Brosimum alicastrum impossible to ignore. That means developing an entire chain – from genetics and seedlings, to plantations and agroforestry, to MayaNut superfood powders and beverages on store shelves.

We want future generations to recognize MayaNut the way people recognize coffee or cacao today: as a flavor, a story, and a living tree that shaped landscapes and communities. This site is the first chapter in that story.

This About section will grow as more field data, images, and plantation milestones come in – including survival maps, flowering and fruiting records, and long-term notes on Brosimum alicastrum genetics and behavior in Florida.

Names, Family & Relatives

Brosimum alicastrum belongs to the Moraceae family – the same deep branch of life that includes figs (Ficus), mulberries (Morus), and breadfruit relatives. Across its range it picks up many names: nuez de ramón, ojoche, breadnut, guayumaro, guaimaro and more.

On this site we use a single unifying name: MayaNut. It is short, easy to say, and hints at the deep cultural history of the tree without being locked into one language.

Range, Climate & Florida Trials

In the wild, Brosimum alicastrum occurs from Mexico through Central America into parts of northern South America and the Caribbean. It is typically a lowland to mid-elevation evergreen, favoring warm, humid climates with minimal frost.

In Florida, the first focus is on frost-free areas at or below roughly 27°N. Above this line, cold snaps become more common and long-term survival is uncertain. The plantings behind The MayaNut Company are designed to map survival, growth rate, flowering and fruiting over many years.

Ecology, Roots & Canopy Function

As a large evergreen canopy tree, MayaNut can provide shade, leaf litter, root structure, and habitat. Its deep roots and strong trunk make it a candidate for hurricane-resilient plantings in certain sites. Much of its ecological behavior in Florida, however, is still being documented tree by tree.

Long term, the question is not just “can it grow?” but “what role does it play?” – in soil protection, microclimate, understory design, and how it interacts with other Moraceae and non-Moraceae species in mixed agroforestry systems.

Genetics, Seedlines & Domestication

One hidden story inside Brosimum alicastrum is genetics: how many distinct seedlines exist, how they differ in fruiting, flavor, drought tolerance, and cold tolerance, and how those traits can be preserved and combined.

The goal of The MayaNut Company is not to create a single “perfect” clone, but a living archive of lines that can fuel future research and selection – while still respecting the species’ wild and traditional context.

Field plantings in South Florida are treated as a living notebook: every seedling, every flush of leaves, every fruiting event adds to a slow, tree-by-tree understanding of how Brosimum alicastrum behaves outside its traditional range.

RECIPES – MAYANUT SUPERFOOD IDEAS

MayaNut Recipes & Superfood Ideas

These early recipes show how roasted MayaNut seed and future powders could move from “experimental tree crop” into daily life – hot drinks, cold blends, porridges, flatbreads, and even a fruit-based jelly from the ripe pulp.

Hot MayaNut Mocha

Drink Comfort

A rich, coffee-adjacent drink using roasted MayaNut plus cocoa. Designed for mornings when you want warmth, flavor, and something that came from a tree older than any café.

  • Whisk MayaNut powder with cocoa and a touch of sugar.
  • Add hot water or milk, froth if possible.
  • Finish with a pinch of cinnamon or vanilla.

MayaNut Breakfast Porridge

Warm Fuel

Imagine a bowl that tastes halfway between toasted grain and soft tree seed. This porridge combines future MayaNut flour with oats or other grains.

  • Cook oats in water or milk as usual.
  • Stir in 1–2 spoonfuls of MayaNut powder near the end.
  • Top with fruit, honey, or nuts for texture.

MayaNut Flatbreads & Tortillas

Savory Everyday

A partial flour replacement, not a full swap. Built to blend with existing recipes and add a subtle tree-based depth.

  • Replace 10–25% of flour with MayaNut flour.
  • Mix and knead as usual for tortillas or flatbreads.
  • Cook on a hot pan; note how aroma changes as they toast.

Cold MayaNut Latte

Iced Summer

A cold drink built for future cafés: iced milk or plant milk, MayaNut powder, a hint of sweetener, and optional coffee or tea.

  • Shake MayaNut powder with cold milk and sweetener.
  • Pour over ice; optionally add a shot of coffee.
  • Top with foam or a dusting of cocoa.

MayaNut Fruit Jelly Concept

Fruit Concept

The ripe MayaNut fruit has sweet pulp around the seed. In regions where fruit is abundant, it could become a jelly or spread that tells the story of the tree.

  • Collect ripe fruits; separate sweet pulp from seed.
  • Cook pulp gently with sugar and a gelling agent.
  • Jar and chill; serve on bread or with cheeses.

Future: MayaNut + Cacao Signature Drink

Flagship Cafe

Long term, the goal is a flagship drink: roasted MayaNut plus cacao, milk, and carefully tuned roasting curves that make “tree to cup” literal.

  • Dial in roasting profiles for both cacao and MayaNut.
  • Extract, blend, and steam like a mocha.
  • Serve as the signature story drink of MayaNut.
FAQ – QUESTIONS ABOUT THE TREE & PROJECT

MayaNut FAQ

These are early answers to questions people ask about the tree, the seed, and the project. On the right, a small FAQ assistant lets you type your own questions about MayaNut, Brosimum alicastrum, Moraceae, and the long-term vision.

What is MayaNut?
MayaNut is the name used here for the roasted, edible seed of Brosimum alicastrum, a tropical evergreen canopy tree in the Moraceae family. People have used the fruits and seeds for centuries as food, drink, and emergency stores.
Where does the tree naturally grow?
It is native to parts of Mexico, Central America, northern South America, and some Caribbean regions. The Florida plantings on this site are part of an experiment – seeing how a historically tropical species behaves at the edge of its comfortable climate.
Is this a coffee replacement?
Not exactly. Roasted MayaNut can be brewed into a coffee-adjacent drink, but the goal is not to replace coffee. The goal is to add a new flavor, story, and tree-based ingredient to the world’s list of everyday foods.
Are there health claims?
No. The MayaNut Company does not provide medical advice or health claims. Any future labels will focus on basic nutrition and flavor, not on miracle promises. Always follow local regulations and talk to health professionals for medical guidance.
Can livestock eat the leaves?
In some regions, the leaves and fruits have been used as fodder, but detailed data for Florida and other new regions is still being gathered. This project treats the tree first as a human food and ecological species; fodder use is a secondary research question.
When will real products be available?
The aim is toward the late 2020s once planted trees reliably fruit and enough seed is available to roast, mill, and test. Until then, the “Store” is conceptual – a window into where this tree could go.
