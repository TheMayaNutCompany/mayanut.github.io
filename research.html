Scope & Methods

This page is an evolving reference library maintained by The MayaNut Company. It organizes core information about MayaNut (Brosimum alicastrum) and documents early cultivation context in Florida through a growing photo archive.

Practical note: this is an early-stage agricultural project. Content is updated as field observations expand and as sources are reviewed.

Origins, Context & Framing

Brosimum alicastrum (MayaNut) is a tropical canopy tree native to Central America and parts of South America and the Caribbean. Across time, communities have used the seed as a durable, storable food—often emphasized in historical accounts during hardship periods—while modern attention has expanded toward agroforestry value and nutritional potential.

Common Names & Indigenous Uses

Brosimum alicastrum has many names. In English and Spanish sources it is frequently referenced as MayaNut / Maya Nut, as well as “ramón” and “ojoche” depending on region. These names refer to the same long-lived canopy tree whose fruit and seed have supported people and wildlife.

Traditional uses include harvesting fruits, roasting seeds, grinding into flour, and preparing a roasted beverage. In some regions, the seed has served as a resilience food because it can be dried and stored.

Natural Range & Climate Tolerance

In the wild, Brosimum alicastrum occurs from southern Mexico through Central America into northern South America. It appears in lowland to foothill forests, from humid evergreen forests to seasonally dry tropical forests.

Once established, deep rooting contributes to drought tolerance. This flexibility is part of why MayaNut is being explored for long-horizon agroforestry systems—including subtropical environments such as South Florida.

Growth Habit, Roots & Hurricanes

MayaNut trees can become large canopy trees in strong conditions, forming a rounded crown and dense evergreen foliage. Juveniles often invest heavily into trunk and root structure before rapid canopy expansion.

Root architecture is a major trait of interest for storm regions: mature trees frequently form strong anchorage, supporting the idea that Brosimum alicastrum may be suitable for hurricane-exposed landscapes.

Fruit appearance can vary by genotype and ripeness; some fruits wrinkle slightly as sugars concentrate—often coinciding with peak sweetness.

Agroforestry & “Anchor Tree” Food Systems

In mixed landscapes, Brosimum alicastrum is commonly used as a dispersed shade tree above crops or pasture, contributing shade, litter, and long-term structure.

For modern systems, the concept is a durable canopy “anchor tree” that persists across decades while understory crops change—supporting a stable agroforestry scaffold while producing seed for food products.

Ecology & Forest Role

Ecologically, MayaNut can function as a habitat and food hub: flowers support insects, fruits feed birds and mammals, and the canopy shapes microclimate beneath the tree.

In restoration settings, integrating Brosimum alicastrum can help rebuild canopy structure, and because people also value the seed, there can be practical incentives to keep trees standing.

Domestication, Selection & Genetics

Domestication of MayaNut in a modern agricultural sense remains early-stage: selecting for traits like seed yield, flavor profile, bearing consistency, and environmental resilience would be core steps. Seed-grown populations are especially valuable for maintaining genetic diversity during selection.

Over time, structured selection and propagation can move the species toward a more standardized crop profile, while still preserving broad genetic capacity for future adaptation.
