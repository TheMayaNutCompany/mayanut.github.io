MayaNut • Brosimum alicastrum • Brosimeae

Research

Brosimum alicastrum (MayaNut / Maya Nut, ramón, ojoche, breadnut) is a tropical canopy tree with deep ecological value and long-term potential as a food and agroforestry crop. This page organizes the core biological, cultural, and evolutionary context — from range and ecology to domestication and the Brosimeae clade.

Origins, Context & “Famine Food” Framing

Brosimum alicastrum is a tropical species native to Central America and South America, as well as parts of the Caribbean. It has primarily been used for thousands of years as a “famine” food, though in recent times is being recognized for its importance as a source of fodder for Mexican ranchers, and it has potential to become the world’s next great superfood.

Common Names & Indigenous Uses

Brosimum alicastrum goes by many names. In English and Spanish, it is often called MayaNut, nuez de ramón, or ojoche. Elsewhere it is known as breadnut, ramón, guayumaro, guaimaro, and more — all referring to the same canopy tree whose fruits and seeds nourish both humans and wildlife.

For many Indigenous communities, the tree has long been more than a curiosity. Fruits are harvested, seeds are roasted, ground, or stored as a reserve. Roasted seeds can produce a hot beverage reminiscent of coffee or cacao, and the flour can be used for tortillas, breads, and porridges. In some regions, families and villages relied on ramón/ojoche as a resilience food during periods of crisis.

Natural Range & Climate Tolerance

In the wild, Brosimum alicastrum occurs from southern Mexico through Central America into northern South America. It is found in lowland and foothill forests, ranging from humid evergreen forests to seasonally dry tropical forests.

Once established, the tree can tolerate drought surprisingly well due to a deep root system. It also thrives in more humid climates, making it a strong candidate for tropical and subtropical agroforestry — including South Florida, where The MayaNut Company concentrates its trials.

Growth Habit, Roots & Hurricanes

MayaNut trees can reach 20–40 m in strong conditions, forming a rounded canopy and dense evergreen foliage. Young trees often invest heavily into roots and trunk structure before strongly expanding canopy size.

Roots are crucial: they frequently anchor into rocky or calcareous substrates, forming a “living anchor.” This is one reason we view Brosimum alicastrum as a promising candidate for hurricane-resilient plantings.

At maturity, fruit skin may wrinkle slightly as the interior pulp becomes extremely sweet — a subtle visual signal that peak ripeness has arrived.

Agroforestry & “Anchor Tree” Food Systems

In traditional agricultural landscapes, Brosimum alicastrum often appears as a dispersed shade tree above crops or pasture. It can coexist with cacao, coffee, fruit trees, or grazing systems — providing shade, leaf litter, and long-term structural stability.

For modern agroforestry, The MayaNut Company views the species as a superfood anchor tree: a durable canopy layer above understory crops that can change over time, while MayaNut trees persist, store carbon, and produce seeds for drinks and flours.

Ecology & Forest Role

Ecologically, MayaNut trees act as activity hubs. Flowers support insects, fruits feed birds and mammals, and the dense canopy creates a cooler, more humid microclimate beneath the tree.

In restoration landscapes, planting Brosimum alicastrum can accelerate the return of canopy structure. Because people also value the seed, there is a practical incentive to keep canopy standing rather than cut it.

Domestication, Genetics & Brosimeae

Brosimum alicastrum belongs to the clade Brosimeae, primarily categorized with four distinct genera: Brosimopsis, Ferolia, Helianthostylis, and Trymatococcus. These four lineages can be traced back to one common ancestor — likely the result of a bottlenecked population in the late Oligocene — but more genetic evidence is needed to understand this history.

Morphologically, Brosimeae and Treculia share nearly identical flower structure and growth habit. In Brosimeae, fusion of peltate bracts was vital in seed dispersal: the fused ripened flower becomes a succulent, syrupy-sweet pulp that monkeys, tapirs, and humans enjoy alike.

In Treculia, hundreds of seeds may be found in a single fruit. Though seeds are elongated, they share multiple traits with Brosimeae seeds. Treculia is placed in Dorsteninae; all other genera in this sub-tribe (excluding Dorstenia) are native to the Eastern Hemisphere.
