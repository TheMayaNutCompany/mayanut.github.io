Research & Botanical Context

This section documents the biological, ecological, cultural, and evolutionary context of Brosimum alicastrum (MayaNut), with a focus on its role within the Brosimeae clade and its long-term potential as a food, agroforestry, and reforestation species.

Origins, Common Names & Traditional Use

Brosimum alicastrum is a tropical tree species native to Central America, South America, and parts of the Caribbean. Across its range, it is known by many names including MayaNut, nuez de ramón, ojoche, breadnut, ramón, guayumaro, and guaimaro. These names all refer to the same canopy tree whose fruits and seeds have fed humans and animals for millennia.

For many Indigenous communities, this tree has long been more than a botanical curiosity. Fruits were harvested seasonally, and seeds were roasted, ground, or stored as a reserve food. Roasted seeds could be prepared as a warm beverage resembling coffee or cacao, or milled into flour for tortillas, breads, and porridges.

Historically, Brosimum alicastrum has often been described as a “famine food” — a resource relied upon during periods of crop failure or social stress. While this framing understates its broader importance, it highlights the species’ exceptional reliability and resilience. In recent decades, the tree has also gained renewed attention for its value as fodder for livestock in Mexico and Central America, as well as its potential to become one of the world’s next major tree-based superfoods.

Natural Range & Climate Tolerance

In the wild, Brosimum alicastrum occurs from southern Mexico through Central America and into northern South America, occupying lowland and foothill forests. It is found in both humid evergreen forests and seasonally dry tropical forests.

Once established, the species shows remarkable drought tolerance due to its deep and extensive root system. At the same time, it performs well in humid environments, making it an excellent candidate for tropical and subtropical agroforestry systems. This ecological flexibility is one reason The MayaNut Company is evaluating the species for long-term cultivation in South Florida.

Growth Habit, Roots & Storm Resilience

Mature MayaNut trees can reach heights of 20–40 meters under favorable conditions, forming a broad, rounded canopy with dense evergreen foliage. Juvenile trees typically invest heavily in root and trunk development before expanding laterally.

The root architecture of Brosimum alicastrum is a defining feature. Roots often anchor into rocky or calcareous substrates, effectively forming a living foundation. This anchoring behavior is one reason the species is being evaluated as a strong candidate for hurricane-resilient plantings in storm-prone regions.

At maturity, the outer skin of the fruit may wrinkle slightly as the interior pulp becomes increasingly sweet — a subtle visual signal marking peak ripeness and nutritional value.

Agroforestry & Food System Integration

In traditional agricultural landscapes, Brosimum alicastrum often appears as a dispersed shade tree above crops or pasture. It commonly coexists with cacao, coffee, fruit trees, or grazing systems, contributing shade, leaf litter, and long-term structural stability.

In modern agroforestry contexts, The MayaNut Company views the species as a “superfood anchor tree” — a durable canopy layer above understory crops that may change over time, while MayaNut trees persist, sequester carbon, and produce seeds for drinks and flours.

Ecology & Forest Role

Ecologically, MayaNut trees function as hubs of activity. Their flowers support insect populations, while fruits feed birds and mammals. The dense canopy creates a cooler, more humid microclimate beneath the tree, benefiting understory species.

In restoration contexts, planting Brosimum alicastrum accelerates the return of canopy cover and structural complexity. Because the seeds also hold direct human value, there is added incentive to preserve mature trees rather than remove them.

Domestication, Genetics & the Brosimeae Clade

On a global scale, Brosimum alicastrum remains in the earliest stages of domestication. While local communities may recognize particularly productive or flavorful trees, there is currently no formal system of named cultivars comparable to mango or citrus.

The species belongs to the clade Brosimeae, which comprises four primary genera: Brosimopsis, Ferolia, Helianthostylis, and Trymatococcus. These lineages trace back to a single common ancestor, likely originating from a population bottleneck during the late Oligocene. Additional genetic evidence is required to fully resolve this evolutionary history.

Morphologically, members of Brosimeae share striking similarities with Treculia. Despite their geographic separation, both exhibit nearly identical flower structure and growth habit. As members of the Moraceae family, the production of inflorescences — clusters of flowers — represents a key evolutionary advantage.

In Brosimeae, the fusion of peltate bracts into a single fleshy structure is especially significant. When ripe, the fused flowers form a succulent, syrupy-sweet pulp consumed by monkeys, tapirs, and humans alike. In Treculia, hundreds of elongated seeds may be found within a single fruit, sharing multiple structural traits with Brosimeae seeds.

Although Treculia belongs to the Dorsteninae subtribe, all other genera in this group (excluding Dorstenia) are native to the Eastern Hemisphere — a biogeographic contrast that continues to intrigue botanists.

The MayaNut Company intentionally sources seed from diverse geographic regions. At least two subspecies are currently recognized — Brosimum alicastrum subsp. alicastrum and Brosimum alicastrum subsp. bolivarense. Preserving this genetic breadth is central to building resilient plantings capable of withstanding pests, disease, and long-term climate change.
