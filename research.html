Brosimum alicastrum is a tropical species native to Central and South America as well as parts of the Caribbean. It has primarily been used for thousands of years as a “famine” food, though in recent times it is being recognized for its importance as a source of fodder for Mexican ranchers, and has potential to become the world’s next great superfood.

Brosimum alicastrum is in the clade Brosimeae, primarily categorized into four distinct genera: Brosimopsis, Ferolia, Helianthostylis, and Trymatococcus. These lineages can be traced back to one common ancestor—possibly following a population bottleneck in the late Oligocene—though more genetic evidence is needed. What is evident is the morphological relationship between Brosimeae and Treculia: despite appearances, they are remarkably similar in flower structure and growth habit. Within Moraceae, producing inflorescences (clusters of flowers) has been an evolutionary advantage; in Brosimeae, the fusion of peltate bracts appears vital for seed dispersal. As the fused, ripened flower becomes a succulent, syrupy-sweet pulp, it is eaten by monkeys, tapirs, and humans alike. In Treculia, hundreds of seeds may occur in a single fruit; although those seeds are elongated, they share many traits with Brosimeae seeds. Within Dorstenieae, most other genera in this group (excluding Dorstenia) are native to the Eastern Hemisphere.

Common names & Indigenous uses

Brosimum alicastrum is known by many names. In English and Spanish it is often called MayaNut, nuez de ramón, or ojoche. Elsewhere you may see breadnut, ramón, guayumaro, guaimaro, and more. All point to the same idea: a canopy tree whose fruits and seeds can feed both people and wildlife.

For many Indigenous communities, the tree has long been more than a curiosity. Fruits are harvested, and the seeds are roasted, milled, or stored as reserves. Roasted seeds can be prepared as a hot drink reminiscent of coffee or cacao, or ground into flour for tortillas, breads, and porridges. In some regions, families and villages have relied on ramón or ojoche as an emergency resilience food during periods of crisis.

Native range & climate

In the wild, Brosimum alicastrum occurs from southern Mexico through Central America into northern South America, and in parts of the Caribbean. It grows in lowland and foothill forests, from humid rainforest to seasonally dry tropical forest.

Once established, the tree tolerates drought surprisingly well thanks to a deep root system, yet it also thrives in wetter climates. This makes it an excellent candidate for tropical and subtropical agroforestry—including in South Florida, where The MayaNut Company is focusing its trials.

Growth, roots & hurricanes

MayaNut trees can reach 20–40 m in favorable conditions, with a rounded crown and dense evergreen foliage. Young trees tend to invest in roots and trunk development before expanding the canopy.

Roots are crucial: they often grip rocky or calcareous soils, forming a living “anchor.” This is one reason we see Brosimum alicastrum as a strong candidate for hurricane-resilient plantings.

When ripe, the fruit skin may wrinkle slightly as the interior pulp becomes very sweet—one small visual signal that the MayaNut season has arrived.

Agroforestry & superfood systems

In traditional agricultural landscapes, Brosimum alicastrum often appears as a scattered shade tree above crops or pasture. It can coexist with cacao, coffee, fruit trees, or grasses—providing shade, leaf litter, and long-term structure.

For modern agroforestry, The MayaNut Company sees the species as a superfood anchor tree: a durable canopy layer above understory crops that can change over time, while MayaNut trees remain, store carbon, and produce seeds for drinks and flours.

Ecology & forest role

Ecologically, MayaNut trees are hubs of activity. Flowers support insects, fruits feed birds and mammals, and the dense canopy creates a cooler, more humid microclimate beneath the tree.

In restoration landscapes, planting Brosimum alicastrum can accelerate the return of shade and structure. Because people also value the seed, there is a real incentive to keep the canopy standing rather than cutting it.

Domestication & genetic diversity

Globally, Brosimum alicastrum is still in the early stages of domestication. Local communities may recognize “good trees” (better flavor or heavier production), but there is not yet a wide program of named cultivars comparable to mango or citrus.

The MayaNut Company intentionally collects seed from many regions. The species includes at least two recognized subspecies—subsp. alicastrum and subsp. bolivarense—and our goal is to preserve this genetic fabric. A broad seed base can create plantings that are more resilient to pests, disease, and climate shifts.
